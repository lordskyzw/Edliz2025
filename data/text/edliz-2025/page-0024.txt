Management of Heart Failure
Maximise the treatment of heart failure by ensuring your patient has an
angiotensin- converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) or an angiotensin receptor
blocker (ARB), beta-blocker, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) e.g
spironolactone, hydralazine/nitrates as well as our usual diuretic (frusemide).
Management of Asthma (Adults and Children)
The management of asthma has been revised to include the use of new
formulations. Please note: it is recommended to use inhaled corticosteroids in all
asthmatics. This has been necessitated by the observation that beta-2 agonists on
their own, do not provide adequate therapy for all asthmatics. Asthma, besides the
bronchospasm, has an inflammatory component which needs to be treated with the
inhaled steroids.
Thus, it is recommended to use the long-acting beta-2 agonists (LABA) together
with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) for the relief of an asthmatic attack. Note: Use
of an inhaled short acting beta-2 agonist (SABA) e.g. inhaled salbutamol on its
own is no longer recommended.
Key Changes in Asthma Management
No Asthmatic Should Be on Salbutamol Monotherapy as:
= Salbutamol (short-acting beta-agonist or SABA) only addresses bronchospasm
without treating underlying airway inflammation.
™ Over-reliance on SABAs has been linked to increased exacerbation risk, poor
asthma control, and higher mortality rates.
What to Do Instead?
Every patient should have a controller containing inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)
or an ICS/LABA (long-acting beta-agonist) combination to manage the
inflammation that drives asthma.
e Symptoms like wheezing or breathlessness signal inflammation in the airways,
which salbutamol alone does not treat.
