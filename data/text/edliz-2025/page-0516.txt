The mainstay of treatment for delirium is to treat the underlying cause(s) and calm
re-orientation. Nutrition and hydration are important. Non-pharmacological
management can include involving familiar relatives in the patient’s care,
provision of orientation aids (e.g. clock, hearing aids, spectacles). It is important to
note that delirium is a distressing state for patients and their relatives and should
therefore be carefully explained, including the risk of recurrence.
Only once all non-pharmacological management has failed, or the patient is at
immediate risk of harm to themselves or others, should sedative medications be
considered. Sedatives should only be prescribed ‘as required’ and not regularly,
always beginning with the lowest effective dose possible to avoid harm.
Haloperidol is generally considered first-line, except in people with movement
disorders in which haloperidol is contra-indicated.

Dementia
Dementia is a syndrome comprising impairment of memory and other higher
cortical functions leading to progressive, irreversible decline in function. The
underlying diseases and clinical features vary according to dementia subtype.
Dementia is a clinical diagnosis, in which a collateral history from a relative
and/or caregiver is key, made by ruling out reversible causes, and can be assisted
with assessment tools (e.g. MoCA, RUDAS). No biochemical or imaging test can
diagnose dementia.
Pharmacological treatment can be trialed in persons with Alzheimer’s Dementia
(AD), which may slow the progression of disease, but is not curative (e.g.
Donepezil). Such medications require specialist prescription and monitoring
(generally in liaison with psychiatry, neurology, or private practice). If after an
initial trial period of 2-3 months there is no effect at the maximum tolerated dose,
it should be discontinued. If benefit appears to have occurred (clinical assessment
+/- cognitive testing), the medication can be continued indefinitely at the
established dose; however, periodic withdrawal (every 18 months) should be
considered to establish whether benefit continues. The evidence of benefit in
advanced AD is weak, therefore donepezil is generally stopped when the patient
reaches this stage
